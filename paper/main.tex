\begin{abstract}
% Số trong abstract lấy từ results/tables (T1 có cột test, T2, T7); sửa cùng lúc với mục IV nếu số đổi.
Published comparisons of retail forecasting models often differ in data, horizons and inputs, which makes their conclusions hard to transfer.
This study benchmarks ten widely used models---ARIMA, SARIMA, SARIMAX, Prophet, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, LightGBM, RNN and LSTM---with seasonal-naive baselines and three forecast-combination schemes.
The data are daily revenue and cost of goods sold of an online retailer for a national total and three regions (3{,}833 days, 2012--2022).
All models receive the same known-in-advance calendar, promotion and regime covariates and forecast directly up to 18 months ahead; recursive variants without calendar information serve as ablations.
Models are compared with four-fold rolling-origin cross-validation, and the selected method is evaluated once on a 549-day hold-out test set with all alternatives, using Diebold--Mariano and Friedman--Nemenyi tests.
On the test set, gradient-boosted trees are the most accurate and cheapest models: XGBoost, Gradient Boosting and LightGBM are statistically indistinguishable, and XGBoost reduces revenue MAE by 23\% relative to a seasonal-naive forecast (MAPE 29\%).
The RNN, which ranked first in cross-validation by a margin of about 1\%, is significantly less accurate than the best model in three of four series.
Calendar information reduces error by 21--84\% relative to recursive models without it, and an indicator of the 2019 level shift is the most valuable covariate group.
The results suggest that how a model receives information about the series matters more than the choice of model family, and that small cross-validation margins between models should not be read as a ranking.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Forecasting total sales is a routine but consequential task in retail.
Daily forecasts drive replenishment, workforce scheduling and cash-flow planning, and their errors propagate into stock-outs, waste and missed revenue~\cite{fildes2022retail}.
Practitioners can choose from three broad families of models:
\begin{itemize}
\item statistical models such as ARIMA and Prophet~\cite{hyndman2021fpp3,taylor2018prophet};
\item machine learning models such as random forests and gradient-boosted trees~\cite{breiman2001rf,chen2016xgboost,ke2017lightgbm};
\item deep learning models such as recurrent networks and LSTM~\cite{hewamalage2021rnn}.
\end{itemize}
Large forecasting competitions, in particular M5 on Walmart sales~\cite{makridakis2022m5}, have shown the strength of tree-based machine learning on retail data~\cite{januschowski2022trees}.
For long horizons, simple models can match far more complex neural architectures~\cite{zeng2023transformers}.
% Đã kiểm 2026-10-29 (literature_matrix.csv): hasan, hobor, retail2023 đọc bản đầy đủ; ensafi, theodoridis chỉ đọc tóm tắt.
Yet applied comparisons on retail data name different winners: ARIMA on Walmart unit sales~\cite{hasan2022comparative}, a stacked LSTM on furniture sales~\cite{ensafi2022seasonal}, deep neural architectures on store-level demand~\cite{theodoridis2025lstmixer} and XGBoost in brick-and-mortar retail~\cite{hobor2026comparative}.

Such conflicting results are hard to interpret because comparisons differ in more than the model, in five ways:
\begin{itemize}
\item models often receive different inputs, for example calendar and promotion information for some but not for others;
\item horizons are often short, for example 28 days~\cite{hasan2022comparative};
\item multi-step strategies differ: machine learning and deep learning models are often run recursively on their own lagged forecasts, so errors accumulate over long horizons, and within one study tree models may be scored one step ahead while neural networks forecast the whole horizon at once~\cite{hobor2026comparative};
\item many studies evaluate on a single train/test split~\cite{hasan2022comparative,retail2023multivariate,hobor2026comparative}, although repeated rolling origins give a more reliable estimate~\cite{tashman2000outofsample,bergmeir2018cv};
\item differences in accuracy are rarely tested for significance~\cite{diebold1995dm,demsar2006statistical}, so small changes in error can be reported as improvements~\cite{retail2023multivariate}.
\end{itemize}
A comparison that controls these factors can say more about the model families themselves.

This study presents such a comparison for daily revenue of an online retailer.
The data combine several difficulties that are common in practice:
\begin{itemize}
\item strong annual seasonality and intra-month patterns;
\item promotion campaigns that recur on fixed calendar dates, some only in odd years;
\item a level shift that lowers revenue by about 40\% from 2019 onwards;
\item four series, a national total and three regions, which raise the question of whether to forecast the total directly or to aggregate regional forecasts~\cite{hyndman2011hierarchical}.
\end{itemize}

We benchmark ten models from three families against two seasonal-naive baselines and three forecast-combination schemes, all under one protocol:
\begin{itemize}
\item every model receives the same covariates, which are known in advance;
\item every model forecasts directly from a single origin, up to 18 months ahead;
\item hyperparameters are tuned and models compared with four-fold rolling-origin cross-validation;
\item the method selected on cross-validation is then evaluated once on a 549-day hold-out test set, together with all alternatives, using significance tests.
\end{itemize}
The study addresses four questions:
(i) which model family is most accurate;
(ii) how much of the accuracy comes from calendar information rather than from the model;
(iii) whether forecast combination improves on the best single model; and
(iv) whether the total is better forecast directly or bottom-up from the regions.

The main contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item A controlled benchmark of statistical, machine learning and deep learning models for daily revenue at horizons of up to 18 months. The protocol was fixed before cross-validation, the proposed method was chosen before the test set was opened, and all later changes are documented.
\item Evidence that gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost, Gradient Boosting, LightGBM) with calendar covariates are the most accurate and cheapest models on the test set, reducing MAE by 23\% relative to a seasonal-naive baseline.
\item Evidence that RNN and LSTM fed the same covariates are competitive in cross-validation but not better on unseen data. The RNN, which ranked first in cross-validation, was significantly less accurate than the best model in three of four series on the test set.
\item An ablation showing what drives accuracy. Calendar-aware direct models reduce error by 21--84\% relative to recursive models without calendar information. An explicit indicator of the 2019 level shift is the most valuable covariate group. Promotion indicators matter mainly during the campaigns themselves.
\end{itemize}
The code, the pre-registered protocol and all result tables are publicly available at \url{https://github.com/myuyen0304/retail-sales-forecasting-research}.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows.
Section~II reviews related work.
Section~III describes the data, covariates, models and evaluation protocol.
Section~IV reports and discusses the results.
Section~V concludes.

\section{Related Work}
% Bài có status "chỉ đọc tóm tắt" trong literature_matrix.csv chỉ được dẫn cho kết luận chính trong tóm tắt,
% không dẫn cho chi tiết thiết kế (horizon, cách chia dữ liệu, kiểm định). Bài "đọc full text" chỉ nhắc ở mức chủ đề.
Retail forecasting has a large literature; Fildes et al.~\cite{fildes2022retail} review research and practice, and Petropoulos et al.~\cite{petropoulos2022theory} survey forecasting methods more broadly.
We focus on the three model families compared in this study, on how forecasts are combined and evaluated, and on what is missing from existing comparisons.

\subsection{Statistical models}
ARIMA models describe a series through its own past values and past errors, and seasonal ARIMA adds the same structure at a seasonal lag~\cite{hyndman2021fpp3}.
For daily data with annual seasonality, a seasonal lag of 365 days makes this impractical.
The usual alternative is dynamic harmonic regression: annual seasonality is represented by Fourier terms, short-term dynamics by ARMA errors, and further known regressors can be added (ARIMAX/SARIMAX)~\cite{hyndman2021fpp3}.
Prophet~\cite{taylor2018prophet} takes a different route.
It decomposes a series into a piecewise trend, Fourier-based seasonal components and holiday effects, fitted as a regression model.
It was designed for business series with strong seasonality and known special days, and it accepts additional regressors.
Both approaches extrapolate a fitted structure and therefore forecast long horizons directly from a single origin.

\subsection{Machine learning}
Random forests~\cite{breiman2001rf} average many decorrelated regression trees.
Gradient boosting~\cite{friedman2001gbm} instead builds trees sequentially, each fitted to the errors of the current ensemble.
XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} and LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm} are scalable, regularised implementations of gradient boosting that are widely used in practice.
Gradient-boosted trees were the basis of most top solutions in the M5 competition on Walmart unit sales~\cite{makridakis2022m5}, and Januschowski et al.~\cite{januschowski2022trees} discuss why tree ensembles work well in forecasting.
Trees do not extrapolate along time, so they forecast either recursively from lagged values or directly from known-in-advance covariates.
For daily retail demand, calendar-related special days are an important input~\cite{huber2020calendric}.
Albassam et al.~\cite{albassam2026tree} compared seven tree-based models on the BigMart and Rossmann data; their ablation identified seasonal and promotional features as critical drivers of accuracy.
Haque et al.~\cite{retail2023multivariate} added macroeconomic indicators to linear and tree-based models for one Walmart store and observed only small changes in error.
Statistical and machine learning methods have also been compared for daily demand at the product (SKU) level~\cite{spiliotis2020sku}.

\subsection{Deep learning}
Recurrent neural networks~\cite{elman1990rnn} process a sequence step by step through a hidden state, and LSTM~\cite{hochreiter1997lstm} adds gates that help retain information over long sequences.
Bandara et al.~\cite{bandara2019lstm} applied LSTM to e-commerce sales demand.
Hewamalage et al.~\cite{hewamalage2021rnn} review RNNs for forecasting and show that their accuracy depends strongly on preprocessing and on design choices such as the input window and the multi-step strategy.
More recent architectures, including Transformers, target long horizons.
However, Zeng et al.~\cite{zeng2023transformers} found that a simple linear model outperformed Transformer-based models on standard long-horizon benchmarks.
On retail data, Theodoridis and Tsadiras~\cite{theodoridis2025lstmixer} found that the Temporal Fusion Transformer, a temporal convolutional network, TSMixer and their proposed LSTM extension (LSTMixer) outperformed common statistical and machine learning methods on sales data from 45 stores.
Qureshi et al.~\cite{qureshi2024rossmann} forecast demand for Rossmann stores with weather covariates and reported that a GRU tuned by grid search improved on a previously proposed LSTM.

\subsection{Forecast combination}
Since Bates and Granger~\cite{bates1969combination}, combining forecasts from different models has been one of the most reliable ways to improve accuracy~\cite{clemen1989combining,wang2023combinations}.
Simple averages are often hard to beat.
Weights estimated from past performance, or learned by stacking~\cite{wolpert1992stacked}, can help when enough out-of-sample forecasts are available.
For classification, the corresponding method is majority voting; for regression it is averaging.
With multiple regions, a total can also be forecast bottom-up, by summing regional forecasts, or by reconciling forecasts across levels of the hierarchy~\cite{hyndman2011hierarchical,wickramasuriya2019mint}.

\subsection{Evaluation of forecasting models}
Evaluation should mimic real use: models are refitted at successive forecast origins and evaluated on the periods that follow (rolling-origin evaluation)~\cite{tashman2000outofsample}.
Standard $k$-fold cross-validation is valid for time series only under specific conditions~\cite{bergmeir2012cv,bergmeir2018cv}.
Error measures have known weaknesses, for example MAPE when values are close to zero, so several measures are usually reported together~\cite{hyndman2006measures}.
Two tests are commonly used to check whether differences in accuracy are larger than chance:
\begin{itemize}
\item the Diebold--Mariano test~\cite{diebold1995dm}, with the small-sample correction of Harvey et al.~\cite{harvey1997hln}, compares two forecasts of the same series;
\item the Friedman test with Nemenyi post-hoc comparisons compares many models over many problems~\cite{demsar2006statistical}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Research gap}
Applied comparisons of these model families on retail data reach different conclusions~\cite{hasan2022comparative,ensafi2022seasonal,theodoridis2025lstmixer,hobor2026comparative}.
As discussed in the introduction, such comparisons rarely control what each model is given, how it forecasts and how it is evaluated.
To our knowledge, few studies on daily retail revenue combine all of the following:
\begin{itemize}
\item all three model families receive the same known-in-advance covariates;
\item every model forecasts directly over horizons of a year or more, so that recursive error accumulation does not decide the comparison;
\item accuracy is evaluated with several rolling origins plus a single hold-out test;
\item differences are tested for significance;
\item an ablation separates the contribution of the model from that of the information it receives.
\end{itemize}
This study addresses that combination for a total series and three regional series.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Data}
% Nguồn mô tả: trang datathon.site (2026-10-29) và data/raw (products.csv, geography.csv, orders.csv).
The data are the order-level transactions of an online fashion retailer in Vietnam, released to participants of DATATHON 2026 (\url{https://www.datathon.site/}), organised by VinTelligence, the Data Science and AI Club of VinUniversity.
They cover 646{,}945 orders and a catalogue of 2{,}412 products in four categories (Streetwear, Outdoor, Casual and GenZ).
Monetary values are in the units of the source data \todo{currency}.
The organisers do not state a public licence, so the raw data are not redistributed with the code.
For this study, the series were extracted from a data warehouse built on the competition data (release \texttt{ccde5a5e}); we verified that all four series and the promotion calendar are identical, to within $1.3\times10^{-8}$, to those rebuilt directly from the raw files.
Daily revenue is $\sum$ quantity $\times$ unit price (before discounts, over orders of all statuses) and daily COGS is $\sum$ quantity $\times$ unit cost.
Each order is assigned to one of three regions (East, Central, West) through its postal code; the regions account for 46.5\%, 30.1\% and 23.4\% of total revenue, and the three regional series sum exactly to the national total.
This gives four series (Total and three regions) and two targets (revenue and COGS), each covering 3{,}833 consecutive days from 4 July 2012 to 31 December 2022 with no missing or non-positive values (Fig.~\ref{fig:series}).

The series combine several features that make long-horizon forecasting difficult.
They have strong annual seasonality and intra-month patterns.
Six promotion campaigns recur on nearly fixed calendar dates: four every year and two (\emph{Urban Blowout} in August and \emph{Rural Special} around February) only in odd years.
There is also a level shift at the end of 2018: mean daily revenue fell from 5.07 million in 2018 to 3.11 million in 2019 ($-39\%$) and stayed at the lower level.

\subsection{Forecasting task}
Let $T$ be a forecast origin, the last day whose value is observed.
Each model is fitted on $y_1,\dots,y_T$ and then produces $\hat y_{T+1},\dots,\hat y_{T+H}$ in a single pass, without re-estimation and without access to actual values inside the horizon.
The horizon is $H=365$ days in cross-validation and $H=549$ days on the test set.
Every model is fitted separately to each of the eight series--target pairs.
All models except the seasonal-naive baselines are fitted to $\log y_t$, and forecasts are transformed back with $\exp(\cdot)$ before evaluation.

\subsection{Covariates}
All covariates are known in advance at the forecast origin (Table~\ref{tab:covariates}).
Day-of-week effects were excluded because they explain almost no variance in the daily series ($R^2=0.008$ in a prior analysis of the same data).
Two variants of the covariate set are used.
The \emph{linear} set is used by SARIMAX and by Prophet, which uses its own yearly seasonality instead of the Fourier terms.
The \emph{tree} set adds ordinal and campaign-progress variables and is used by the tree ensembles and by RNN/LSTM.

Two rules prevent information from after the origin leaking into the covariates.
First, campaign windows for future dates are extrapolated from the calendar dates of campaigns that started on or before the origin; a campaign observed at least twice and only in odd years is switched on only in odd years.
Second, the post-2019 indicator is included only when at least 180 days after 1 January 2019 have been observed, i.e.\ for origins from mid-2019 onwards (folds F3, F4 and the test set).
The break date itself comes from exploratory analysis of the full series in a previous project; we return to this in the discussion of limitations.

\subsection{Models}
Table~\ref{tab:models} lists the ten main models, grouped into three families, and the two baselines.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Baselines.} Seasonal naive forecasts copy the value on the same calendar day one year earlier (SNaive-1Y) or two years earlier (SNaive-2Y). SNaive-2Y keeps the odd/even-year parity of the odd-year campaigns.
\item \emph{Statistical models.} ARIMA uses no covariates; its order is chosen by AIC over $p,q\in\{0,1,2\}$, $d\in\{0,1\}$, with a constant when $d=0$. A seasonal ARIMA with period 365 is impractical, so SARIMA is implemented as dynamic harmonic regression, i.e.\ Fourier terms with ARMA errors~\cite{hyndman2021fpp3}. SARIMAX extends it with the full linear covariate set. Prophet~\cite{taylor2018prophet} uses its yearly seasonality plus the linear covariates (except Fourier terms) as additive regressors, with weekly and daily seasonality disabled.
\item \emph{Machine learning.} Random Forest~\cite{breiman2001rf}, histogram-based Gradient Boosting~\cite{friedman2001gbm} (scikit-learn), XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} and LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm} regress $\log y_t$ directly on the covariates of day $t$. They use no lagged values of the target, so a single fitted model produces forecasts for any horizon and errors do not accumulate along the horizon.
\item \emph{Deep learning.} The Elman RNN~\cite{elman1990rnn} and LSTM~\cite{hochreiter1997lstm} take a window of the standardized tree covariates of the $L$ days ending at $t$. One recurrent layer and a linear output layer map this window to the standardized $\log y_t$, again without lagged target values. Networks are trained with Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size 64, mean squared error). The number of epochs is chosen by early stopping on the last 10\% of the training period (patience 25, at most 300 epochs), and the network is then refitted on the full training period for that number of epochs. Each network is trained with five seeds; the reported forecast is the mean of the five seed forecasts, and the spread across seeds is reported separately.
\end{itemize}

To show where the accuracy of the main models comes from, we also run two kinds of ablation.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Pure variants} use no calendar information and forecast recursively, with default (untuned) hyperparameters:
\begin{itemize}
\item SARIMA with weekly seasonality, $(1,1,1)\times(1,0,1)_7$;
\item Prophet with default settings;
\item each tree model on target lags $\{1,2,3,7,14,28,30,60,90,180,365\}$ and rolling means over $\{7,28,90\}$ days;
\item RNN and LSTM on the last 56 values of the target (three seeds).
\end{itemize}
\item \emph{Feature-group ablations} remove one covariate group (regime, promo or dom; Table~\ref{tab:covariates}) from SARIMAX and from LightGBM and keep their tuned hyperparameters.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hyperparameter tuning}
Each tuned model has a grid of at most 12 configurations, fixed before tuning (Table~\ref{tab:models}).
Every configuration is evaluated on the Total revenue series over the four cross-validation folds with one seed.
The configuration with the lowest mean MAE is then used for all series and targets.
The test set is never used for tuning or for any other choice.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
We use rolling-origin evaluation with an expanding training window~\cite{tashman2000outofsample,bergmeir2018cv} (Fig.~\ref{fig:splits}).
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Cross-validation} has four folds, with origins on 30 June of 2017, 2018, 2019 and 2020. Each fold scores the 365 days after its origin, and training always starts on 4 July 2012. Fold F2 contains the 2018 level shift.
\item \emph{Test.} The test origin is 30 June 2021, and the test set (1 July 2021 to 31 December 2022, 549 days) was forecast and scored once, after the proposed method and the combination rules had been fixed.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{F03_splits.pdf}
\caption{Expanding-window cross-validation folds and hold-out test set.}\label{fig:splits}
\end{figure}

Accuracy is measured on the original scale over the $n$ scored days:
\begin{gather*}
\mathrm{MAE}=\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_t|y_t-\hat y_t|,\qquad
\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_t(y_t-\hat y_t)^2},\\
\mathrm{MAPE}=\tfrac{100}{n}\textstyle\sum_t|y_t-\hat y_t|/y_t .
\end{gather*}
MAPE is well defined because all values are positive.
Errors are also broken down by lead time (1--30, 31--90, 91--365 and 366--549 days) and for the odd-year August campaign.

The folds share training data and are therefore not independent.
For this reason we report the mean and standard deviation across folds, and do not use paired $t$-tests across folds.
Models are compared with two statistical tests:
\begin{itemize}
\item On cross-validation, the Friedman test with the Nemenyi post-hoc test~\cite{demsar2006statistical} ($\alpha=0.05$) ranks the ten main models and two baselines over the 16 revenue problems (four series $\times$ four folds).
\item On the test set, each model is compared with the most accurate model of the same series and target using the Diebold--Mariano test~\cite{diebold1995dm} on absolute errors, with the small-sample correction of Harvey et al.~\cite{harvey1997hln}. Errors along a forecast path are autocorrelated, so the long-run variance of the loss differential is estimated with the Newey--West estimator (Bartlett kernel, $\lfloor n^{1/3}\rfloor=8$ lags for $n=549$), and $p$-values are taken from the $t_{n-1}$ distribution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Forecast combination}
Majority voting applies to classification; its regression analogue is forecast averaging.
We combine the ten main models in three ways:
\begin{itemize}
\item the equal-weight average of the three models with the lowest MAE;
\item inverse-MAE weights over the same three models;
\item stacking~\cite{wolpert1992stacked} by non-negative least squares on out-of-fold forecasts, with weights normalised to sum to one.
\end{itemize}
To avoid look-ahead, the weights for fold F$k$ are learned only from the forecasts of the folds before it. F1 has no earlier fold and therefore uses the equal-weight average of all ten models. Weights for the test set are learned from all four folds.

Comparing a combination with the single model that turns out best in hindsight would favour the single model.
Instead, the reference is a single model chosen by the same online rule, namely the model with the lowest MAE on the earlier folds.
Combinations are therefore compared on folds F2--F4.

\subsection{Multi-region and COGS forecasts}
For each model, the \emph{bottom-up} forecast of the total is the sum of its three regional forecasts, and it is compared with the model's direct forecast of the total~\cite{hyndman2011hierarchical}.
COGS is forecast directly, and also indirectly as forecast revenue multiplied by the COGS-to-revenue ratio for each month and for the presence of the August campaign, learned on the training period.

\subsection{Choice of the proposed method}
The selection rule was fixed in the protocol before cross-validation: the proposed method is the model with the lowest mean CV MAE on revenue, averaged over the four series.
This rule selected the RNN (mean of five seeds), and the choice was recorded before the test set was opened.
The test set is nonetheless reported for every model, so that the selected method can be compared with all alternatives on unseen data.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\subsection{Setup}
Cross-validation, test forecasts and the tuning of RNN/LSTM were run on Google Colab (2 vCPUs, CPU only).
The software stack was Python 3.13, statsmodels 0.15.0, Prophet 1.5.0, scikit-learn 1.6.1, XGBoost 3.4.1, LightGBM 4.6.0 and PyTorch 2.11.0.
The statistical and tree-based models were tuned beforehand on a 12-core laptop (Python 3.13.5).
The median time to fit one model to one series, target and fold was:
\begin{itemize}
\item 1.1\,s for XGBoost, 1.3\,s for Gradient Boosting and 2.5\,s for LightGBM;
\item 16\,s for Random Forest and 66\,s for SARIMAX;
\item 35\,s per seed for the RNN and 88\,s per seed for the LSTM, so five-seed RNN and LSTM forecasts cost about 160 and 400 times as much as XGBoost.
\end{itemize}
Every one of the 1{,}216 CV tasks and 304 test tasks finished without error.

\subsection{Cross-validation results}
Table~\ref{tab:main} (left) reports revenue MAE averaged over the four CV folds.
The five best models form a tight group.
Averaged over the four series, their MAE is:
\begin{itemize}
\item RNN 508 thousand, which is the lowest;
\item XGBoost 512, LSTM 513 and LightGBM 514;
\item Gradient Boosting 517.
\end{itemize}
These five are followed by Prophet and SARIMAX (536--537) and Random Forest (567).
All of them clearly improve on the seasonal-naive baseline (698).
ARIMA (1{,}454) and SARIMA (846) are worse than SNaive-1Y.
The Friedman test rejects equal performance over the 16 revenue problems ($p=4.6\times10^{-24}$; Fig.~\ref{fig:cd}).
However, the Nemenyi critical difference ($\mathrm{CD}=4.17$) is wide.
All eight covariate-based models lie within one CD of the best average rank, while the baselines, SARIMA and ARIMA are significantly worse.
Fold F2, which contains the level shift, dominates the errors: its MAE is about 750 thousand for the leading models, against 316--532 thousand in the other folds.

The RNN's advantage depends on seed averaging.
Each RNN seed on its own has a mean MAE of $522\pm17$ thousand, which is worse than a single XGBoost fit (512).
Only the average of the five seed forecasts reaches 508.
The fold closest to the test period also points in a different direction: on F4, XGBoost (316) and LightGBM (317) are ahead of RNN (338) and LSTM (360).

\subsection{Test-set results}
Applying the rule of Section~III to the CV results selected the RNN as the proposed method.
The test set, however, ranks the models differently (Table~\ref{tab:main}, right; Fig.~\ref{fig:test}).
\begin{itemize}
\item The three gradient-boosted tree models are the most accurate: XGBoost has an average MAE of 325 thousand (MAPE 29.2\%), Gradient Boosting 326 and LightGBM 327.
\item NNLS stacking follows with 328.
\item The RNN reaches 337 (3.7\% above XGBoost), and a single RNN seed reaches $344\pm4$.
\item The LSTM reaches 348.
\item XGBoost reduces MAE by 23\% relative to SNaive-1Y (423) and by 22\% relative to SNaive-2Y (416).
\end{itemize}
The Diebold--Mariano tests in Table~\ref{tab:dm} compare each model with the most accurate forecast of each series:
\begin{itemize}
\item None of XGBoost, Gradient Boosting, LightGBM and NNLS stacking is significantly less accurate than the best forecast ($p\ge0.07$).
\item The RNN is significantly less accurate in three of the four series ($p=0.013$--$0.029$), and in the fourth (West) $p=0.064$.
\item The LSTM and Random Forest are significantly worse in all four series.
\end{itemize}
The proposed method thus ranked first on CV but did not keep that position on unseen data.
This agrees with its weaker result on F4 and with how small its CV margin was.

Errors do not grow with the horizon for the direct models.
For XGBoost, the test MAE (mean of four series) is 292 thousand for leads 1--30, 313 for 31--90, 355 for 91--365 and 289 for 366--549.
The higher error at 91--365 days coincides with the volatile spring of 2022 (Fig.~\ref{fig:test}) rather than reflecting distance from the origin.
By contrast, the recursive pure variants degrade sharply with the horizon (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).
In the odd-year August campaign of 2021, SNaive-1Y copies a non-campaign year and its MAE rises to 893 thousand, while the main covariate-based models stay between 263 (Prophet) and 348 thousand (Random Forest).

\subsection{Value of calendar information}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} (top) compares each calendar-aware model with its pure recursive counterpart.
\begin{itemize}
\item Calendar information reduces revenue MAE by 21--78\% on CV and by 21--84\% on the test set.
\item The calendar-aware version is better in 14 to 16 of the 16 series--fold combinations for every model.
\item The gains are largest for the networks (65--84\%) and for SARIMA versus SARIMAX (63--71\%). Their recursive versions drift away from the level of the series over a horizon of one year or more.
\item For the tree models, the gains are 24--36\%.
\end{itemize}
These gains are upper bounds on the value of calendar information, because the pure variants were not tuned.

The feature-group ablation (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, bottom) shows which information matters:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Regime indicator.} Removing it increases the F3--F4 MAE by 147\% for LightGBM and by 69\% for SARIMAX. On the test set, LightGBM without the indicator is still twice as inaccurate ($+101\%$), whereas SARIMAX is unaffected ($-2\%$).
\item \emph{Intra-month variables.} They matter for SARIMAX ($+23\%$ on CV, $+24\%$ on test) but not for LightGBM ($+1\%$). The tree set still contains day of year and month, so LightGBM can recover the position within the month from them.
\item \emph{Promotion indicators.} Removing them changes the overall MAE by at most 2\%, because the campaigns recur on fixed dates and are largely absorbed by the seasonal terms. The exception is the odd-year campaign: in August 2021, removing them raises the MAE of LightGBM from 295 to 445 thousand and of SARIMAX from 282 to 386 thousand. Promotion information therefore matters locally even though it barely moves the overall error.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Forecast combination}
Combinations are judged against the online-selection benchmark on folds F2--F4.
There, the average of the top three models reaches a mean MAE of 504 thousand, against 506 for online selection.
Its rank is the best among all models (4.67 versus 5.58), but the difference is well within the Nemenyi CD of 6.66.
On the test set, NNLS stacking (328) improves on online selection (336), which picked the RNN for three of the four series.
The equal-weight and inverse-MAE combinations (331) are close to each other because their weights are nearly equal.
No combination is more accurate than the best single model on test (XGBoost, 325).
Forecast combination therefore mainly protects against choosing a weaker model.

\subsection{Multi-region and COGS}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Bottom-up versus direct.} Summing the three regional forecasts gives a lower CV MAE for Total revenue than forecasting the total directly, for every main model. The gain is 1.3--4.0\% for the calendar-aware models, 5.9\% for SARIMA and 21.6\% for ARIMA.
\item \emph{COGS direct versus ratio.} Forecasting COGS directly is more accurate than scaling the revenue forecast by a seasonal COGS-to-revenue ratio, by 0.5--4.0\% across the calendar-aware models.
\item \emph{COGS on the test set.} The best COGS forecasts come from LightGBM without promotion indicators (average MAE 294 thousand) and XGBoost (305).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Discussion}
Three findings stand out.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Gradient-boosted trees.} With calendar covariates and a direct (non-recursive) strategy, these trees are the most accurate and cheapest option for daily revenue forecasts up to 18 months ahead. RNN and LSTM fed the same covariates are competitive on CV but not better on unseen data, and need several seeds to reach that level. This agrees with the strength of tree ensembles in retail competitions~\cite{makridakis2022m5,januschowski2022trees} and in brick-and-mortar retail~\cite{hobor2026comparative}. Studies in which deep models won~\cite{ensafi2022seasonal,theodoridis2025lstmixer} used different data and protocols, so their results are not directly comparable with ours.
\item \emph{What drives accuracy.} How a model receives information about the series matters more than which model is used. On the test set, each of the eight calendar-aware models in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} beats every pure recursive variant, and the regime indicator is the most valuable single covariate group. Together with SARIMAX's insensitivity to it on test, this suggests that the benefit of the indicator depends on how the model represents the level of the series. Unlike the Rossmann ablation of Albassam et al.~\cite{albassam2026tree}, promotion indicators barely change the overall error here, because the campaigns recur on fixed dates that the seasonal terms already capture.
\item \emph{Rankings among close models are unstable.} A margin of 1\% on CV did not survive the test period. Benchmarks should therefore report significance tests and treat models within the critical difference as equivalent.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The study covers a single retailer and a single test origin.
\item It is a retrospective benchmark. The covariate set and the date of the level shift come from a previous analysis of the same data whose backtest overlapped the test period, so the test set was not completely unseen during model development.
\item Hyperparameters were tuned on Total revenue only and applied to all series and targets.
\item The pure variants were not tuned.
\item The variance estimator of the Diebold--Mariano test was corrected after the test results were first computed, to account for autocorrelated daily errors. This weakened one of the four RNN comparisons from significant to non-significant (West), and we report the corrected values.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
This study compared ten statistical, machine learning and deep learning models for daily retail revenue at horizons of up to 18 months.
All models received the same known-in-advance covariates and forecast directly, and they were evaluated with a fixed protocol: rolling-origin cross-validation, a single hold-out test and significance tests.

Gradient-boosted trees were the most accurate models on the test set and also the cheapest to fit.
XGBoost, Gradient Boosting and LightGBM could not be separated statistically, and XGBoost reduced MAE by 23\% relative to the seasonal-naive baseline.
RNN and LSTM with the same inputs were competitive in cross-validation.
The RNN selected there was, however, significantly less accurate than the best model in three of four series on the test set, and its cross-validation advantage relied on averaging five seeds.
Forecast combination protected against choosing a weaker model but did not improve on the best one.
Forecasting the total bottom-up from the regions was slightly more accurate than forecasting it directly, and COGS was better forecast directly than through a revenue ratio.

The ablations showed what drives accuracy.
Known-in-advance calendar information and a direct forecasting strategy reduced error by 21--84\% compared with recursive models without this information.
An explicit indicator of the level shift was the most valuable single covariate group, while promotion indicators mattered mainly during the campaigns themselves.

For practitioners, these results support three recommendations:
\begin{itemize}
\item use a gradient-boosted tree model with calendar, promotion and regime covariates as a strong and inexpensive default for long-horizon daily revenue forecasts;
\item invest in encoding what is known about the calendar and about structural changes before investing in more complex models;
\item treat small differences in cross-validation accuracy as ties unless they are confirmed by significance tests on held-out data.
\end{itemize}

The study has the limitations discussed in Section~IV: a single retailer, a single test origin and a retrospective design.
Future work should do three things:
\begin{itemize}
\item repeat the protocol on data from other retailers and with several test origins;
\item detect level shifts automatically instead of fixing their date in advance;
\item evaluate global models trained jointly on the regional series and reconciliation methods for the regional hierarchy~\cite{wickramasuriya2019mint}.
\end{itemize}
